1 Introduction

[P0001] p.1 unknown §1 Introduction
Gas hydrate is an ice-like clathrate in which methane molecules occupy cages formed by water molecules under high pressure and low temperature. In marine sediments, the base of the hydrate stability zone (HSZ) is commonly marked by a bottom-simulating reflector (BSR) on seismic profiles. Because the stability of gas hydrate depends on pressure, temperature, pore-water salinity and gas composition, small changes in these conditions can shift the HSZ boundary by tens of metres.

2 Materials and Methods

[P0002] p.1 unknown §2 Materials and Methods
We recovered pressure cores from site CUG-3 at a water depth of 1180 m. Pore-water salinity was measured by refractometry and is reported as 3.5 wt% NaCl equivalent. Subsamples were equilibrated at 1.2 MPa and 4 °C inside a high-pressure cell, and the dissociation pressure was recorded with a resolution of 0.01 MPa. Porosity was derived from gamma-ray density logs, and permeability was estimated from the Kozeny–Carman relation, Eq. (1).

Hydrate saturation in the sandy interval ranges from 21% to 64% of pore volume. The BSR occurs 12 m below the seafloor at the summit of the ridge and deepens to 35 m on the flank. 4 Discussion

[P0004] p.1 unknown §4 Discussion
The mismatch between the modelled HSZ thickness and the observed BSR depth is 6 m at site CUG-3. We attribute this mismatch to pore-water freshening caused by hydrate dissociation, rather than to a regional heat-flow anomaly. Because the permeability of the sandy layer is one order of magnitude higher than that of the overlying clay, fluid flow may redistribute salinity over lateral distances of several kilometres (Sloan & Koh, 2008). This mechanism does not, however, explain the sharp BSR on the flank, where the sediment is clay-rich.

Gas hydrate stability at site CUG-3 is controlled by the coupled effects of pressure, temperature and pore-water salinity.
